Caps Formalism — What “Deducible Certainty” Actually Means

An advanced-player article. Last updated 2026-05-08.

The Caps section of the rules tells you when to call. This article tells you why. It is a precise account of the test scrutiny applies, written for intermediate and expert players who want to argue Caps from first principles. Beginners can ignore it: the headline rule (“in every distribution of cards consistent with what I know, I have some line of play — possibly reacting to what opps do as the trick-taking unfolds — that wins every remaining round”) does the work for normal play. The formalism only matters when a borderline situation goes to scrutiny, or when you want to step through possible worlds at the table to convince yourself a call holds.

Why Caps is hard, in one paragraph

Caps is not a fact about the deal. It is a fact about your information set — what you, sitting in your seat, can deduce from your own hand plus the public record of play. Two players at the same table can become Caps-obligated at different moments, because they know different things. The Trumper, who has seen every folded card revealed at end of round, often knows more than their partner; the external team usually knows least. A naive test — “given the actual deal, is there a winning line?” — is the wrong test. That asks whether an omniscient observer sees a sweep, not whether you can deduce one. In bridge terminology that is double-dummy analysis on the actual deal, and it is too generous: it gives Caps to a player who, given what they actually know, could still lose. The correct test is single-dummy: for every deal consistent with what you know, do you have a (possibly deal-dependent, adaptive) strategy that wins against every legal continuation by the other seats? Single-dummy is strictly stricter, and it is the only test that matches the rules’ phrase “given all information available to them, irrespective of how any other player chooses to play.”

What you know

Your information set at any moment is the union of:

  1. Your own hand.
  2. Your own play history — including face-down plays you made, since you know what you put down.
  3. The public face-up history — every face-up card played by anyone, in completed rounds and the in-progress round.
  4. Public face-down revelations — folded trumps revealed at end-of-round resolution become public the moment they are flipped.
  5. Public suit-exhaustion — every player who failed to follow a led suit is publicly known to be out of that suit from then on, because the closed-trump rule mandates folding only when one cannot follow.
  6. The Trumper’s privileged observations — if you are the Trumper, you saw the identity of every face-down card at end of round, including opposition cuts, opposition minuses, and your partner’s minuses. Your partner and the opposition did not.

Note the asymmetry. The Trumper’s information set is larger than anyone else’s, which is exactly why the rules hold the Trumper to a stricter “first opportunity” standard (§C-4). The external team’s information set excludes line 6, which is why scrutiny is more lenient on them (§C-12). The same predicate, evaluated against a smaller information set, automatically becomes more lenient — there is no special case.

The space of consistent worlds

A world is a hypothesis about every card whose location is currently hidden from you. It assigns an identity to every card in every other seat’s hand and to every face-down completed-round card whose value you don’t already know. A world is consistent with your information if:

  • Cards conserve. Every card in the 32-card pack appears in exactly one place.
  • Hand sizes are right. Each remaining seat has the right number of cards for the round number.
  • Suit-exhaustion holds. No seat known to be out of suit s is given a card of suit s.
  • Hidden minuses obey the rules. A folded card whose identity you don’t know cannot be a card the player could have followed with — neither the led suit (they would have followed) nor, in most cases, trump (a trump fold would have been revealed at round end).
  • Known cards stay put. Cards whose identity you have already seen appear where you saw them.

The set of consistent worlds is finite. Early in a game it is large; mid-to-late game, suit-exhaustion typically prunes it heavily. The actual deal is always one of these worlds — but only one.

The Caps obligation, formally

You are Caps-obligated at the current state if and only if:

  1. Precondition. Your team has won every round so far.
  2. For every world W consistent with your information,
  3. there exists an adaptive strategy σW — a function from observed play to your next legal card —
  4. such that for every legal continuation by the other seats (including the worst conceivable play),
  5. the playout from now — with you playing σW in turn and the others playing freely — ends with your team winning every remaining round.

This is the adaptive “for every world there exists a strategy” (∀W ∃σW) formulation. Different worlds may be witnessed by different strategies; within a world, your play may branch on what opps reveal as the call sequence unfolds. This is strictly more permissive than the older “single fixed order that works in every world” formulation (∃O ∀W) — some genuinely cap-callable hands have no fixed order, only a per-world adaptive strategy.

Three subtleties matter at the table:

  • You are not committed to a fixed order. When you call Caps, you expose your hand and may state one demonstration line for clarity. That line is one trace through your strategy under one plausible opp continuation — not a binding sequence. If opps play differently than your demo line anticipated, you continue with whatever your strategy prescribes for what they actually played. Scrutiny verifies the existence of a covering family {σW}, not the prescience of any single trace.
  • No “reasonable” assumption. Opponents may play their absolute worst card every turn. The rules say “irrespective of how any other player chooses to play,” and the formal test takes that literally.
  • Forced plays count. If a world or the rules force a unique legal play (your partner has only one card of the led suit, say), the universal quantifier over opponent strategies collapses to that single play. This is how “deducible certainty via partner” works — not a special case, just a consequence of quantifying over legal moves.
  • Adaptive does not weaken “deducible certainty.” Your strategy may only branch on cards actually played, not on partner choices you hope for. Where partner has discretion, your strategy must succeed against every legal partner choice (partner is treated as part of the adversarial completion for the obligation test).

Adaptive — codified at this group’s table Earlier wordings of this rule required a single fixed order of play, committed at the moment of the call. In practice the table has long validated calls where the caller demonstrated they could win against any line opps might choose — sometimes branching mid-line (“if east plays X I play Y; if east plays Z I play W”). The 2026 rules revision formalised that practice by replacing ∃O ∀W with ∀W ∃σW.

First opportunity and timing

Your information only changes on observation events — when a card is played, when round resolution flips a folded trump, when (as Trumper) you inspect folded cards at end of round. Between events, your information set is static, so your Caps obligation cannot flip from false to true.

The first obligation state is the earliest such event at which the obligation predicate becomes true. The Late Caps judgement is then a function of when you actually called, relative to that first obligation state, under one of three timing policies:

  • Strict. The call must be placed at the first obligation state, before any further observation event.
  • Lenient (rules default, §C-3). The call may be placed at any moment up to and including your next own play. You may not let one of your own plays go by without calling.
  • Unified-time. A configurable wall-clock window after the first obligation state — used in fast play, not in scrutiny.

Scrutiny applies the lenient policy by default. The strict policy is the analytical ideal but rarely enforced in practice.

One predicate, four mechanics

The same machinery, with different parameters, expresses every related rule:

MechanicSpecialisation
CapsYour team is the trumping team; if you are the Trumper, your information set includes folded-card observations.
External CapsYour team is the non-trumping team; the information set excludes folded-card observations. Same predicate, smaller info set, automatically more lenient.
Absolute HandCaps obligation evaluated at the start of round 1, before any plays. Your information is your hand and the public bidding history.
Claim BalanceReplace “win every remaining round” with “reach the points threshold.” World enumeration is identical.

Absolute Hand is the round-1 case of Caps. Claim Balance is Caps with a different terminal predicate. One framework, four mechanics.

Walking the worlds at the table

You don’t enumerate worlds explicitly in real play — you reason about the structure of the world set: “all remaining tops are mine; opponents are out of the only suit I lack; my partner’s last card is forced.” The formalism is what scrutiny appeals to when an argument is contested. To rehearse the discipline, work the worked example in the Caps section through these steps:

  1. Inventory. List the cards still unaccounted for — the cards in the pack minus your hand minus the public record (and, if you are the Trumper, minus what you saw face-down).
  2. Constrain. Apply suit-exhaustion. Drop any seat-suit pair that the public record forbids.
  3. World-walk. For each remaining world W, ask: “do I have some line of play — possibly branching on what opps play next — that wins every remaining round in this world?” If yes for all worlds, you have a covering strategy.
  4. Demonstration. Pick one trace through your strategy as your demo line — typically the most informative opener, or the line that handles the trickiest world. State it for clarity, knowing you are not bound to it.
  5. Stress-test. If any step depends on a partner’s choice rather than a forced play, you are not yet obligated. Wait for the next observation event and try again.

If every consistent world admits a winning line for you, and every “but what if the opponent holds X” challenge can be answered by either “that world is impossible (rule the public record forbids it)” or “in that world I play Y and still sweep”, you have Caps. If even one world admits a legal opp line you can’t answer, you don’t yet.

Two ways to lose a Caps argument under scrutiny

In practice, contested Caps calls fail in one of two ways:

  • A consistent world admits no winning strategy. The opposition produces a card distribution that respects everything publicly known and in which no line of yours wins all remaining rounds — opps have a legal play that takes a trick no matter what you do. The call collapses. (This is most common when the caller has tacitly assumed the location of a key card — e.g. the last trump — that the public record does not pin down.)
  • A legal opp strategy breaks every line in some world. The world is fixed but the opp demonstrates a legal play sequence that beats a round against every continuation you might try — typically a discard or fold the caller did not consider. (This is more common in External Caps, where the caller has less visibility and may have miscounted exhaustion.)

Both failures point at the same root cause: in some consistent world, no covering strategy exists. The formal test is whether every world has its own winning strategy.

In the broader literature, this is known as single-dummy claim adjudication: the contract-bridge analogue is Law 70 of the Laws of Duplicate Bridge, and the canonical algorithmic treatment is Frank, Basin & Bundy, “Single-Dummy Solving” (AAAI 1992) — whose vanilla per-world double-dummy intersection is exactly this document’s predicate. 304 is stricter than bridge in adversary scope: bridge requires a claim to hold against any reasonable line; 304 requires it to hold against any line whatsoever.
